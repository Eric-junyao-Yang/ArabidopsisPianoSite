Sonifying transcription

Arabidopsis Synthesizer

Have you ever looked at a wiggly graph and wondered what would happen if you plug the shape into a synthesizer? Who hasn't?

Transcription measurements reflect gene activity, which can be affected by external or internal stimuli. Yuchen Yang and colleagues measured the effect of the day/night cycle on each of the roughly 27,000 genes in Arabidopsis thaliana. This interactive data "sonification" explores this experimental dataset using sound .

The Science How gene expression becomes an instrument

Arabidopsis thaliana is a plant that scientists love to study. Yuchen Yang and colleagues grew Arabidopsis under 16hr light and 8hr dark cycles, and measured the activity of each of the 27,000 genes every three hours for two days.

Here is TOC1 (AT5G61380), a known circadian gene, alongside PP2AA3 (AT1G13320), a housekeeping (non-oscillating) gene:

We can “hear” the data if we treat the resulting plot as an audio waveform.

An audio waveform is a representation of sound:

For the synthesizer below, amplitude is locked and frequency is mapped to the keyboard, so you can focus on waveform shape. That gives about 27,000 “instruments” derived from the Arabidopsis genome.

How would we expect oscillating and non-oscillating genes to sound different? You can explore the interactive synthesizer below to figure it out for yourself, or go to the discussion section below for spoilers.

How To Quick guide and orientation

Picking a Sound

There are two linked plots below grouped by Biology vs Sound. Each point is a gene. Click to select. Click away to cancel. Hover highlights the same gene in both views. Other parts of the dashboard will update as you select a gene.

Playing a Sound

Click the virtual keyboard at the bottom or use the corresponding keys on your keyboard to play the selected gene's waveform. Polyphony is supported (i.e. multiple notes at once). The up and down arrows to the right control the octave.

Coloring the Graph

The best way to explore the plots is by coloring them. Selecting any of the coloring modes updates the plots. As you change colors, the hint box below will update to give more details.

Filtering

Filtering is a great way to explore the colored graph dynamically. Here is a . The vertical plot between the two larger plots is called a violin plot. The top and bottom circles can be clicked and dragged to set the upper and lower filtering thresholds. The body of the window can be dragged and repositioned as well. The filter updates based on the coloring mode.

Energy %

Below the three plots is a narrow horizontal plot showing the energy % of the selected wave's component frequencies. The frequency components strongly affect how the waveform sounds. The stronger the high frequency components (periods shorter than 24 hr), the "brighter" the sound. The discussion below has more details.

Waveform

All Genes Mode

The waveform above the keyboard displays the experimental data of the selected gene. The line is an interpolation through the data. The interpolation is used to generate the sounds you hear.

Circadian Mode

Instead of simple interpolation, oscillating genes can be modeled. When you play a sound, the model generates one possible waveform that fits the experimental data every half a second. The green line is the average and the green band is the 95% confidence interval.

All genes vs Circadian

Display all genes or only circadian ones. Some coloring modes change subtly, so keep an eye on the help text. The waveform (and sound) also changes. The sound you hear morphs between the sampled waveforms, so the wobble you hear matches the certainty of the shape.

Jukebox

              Discussion Oscillating data and sound quality

              Graph Grouping

              These plots are called UMAPs, and data points that are similar are grouped together.

              All Genes Mode

              In "Group by Biology", you will notice the organizing principles are "peak-time (phase)" and oscillation energy. In "Group by Sound", phase information is discarded because we assume the waveform repeats indefinitely, so the plot is organized by oscillation energy only.

              Circadian Mode

              "Group by Biology" is still strongly organized by "peak-time (phase)", but "Group by Sound" no longer groups well because now every gene has strong 24hr oscillation energy.

              Oscillating Genes

              The vast majority of the oscillating genes oscillate at around 24 hours. A smaller group oscillates at 12 hours. Genes that don't seem to repeat their pattern will have a strong 48hr oscillation component, because that's how long the experiment lasted.

              Sound Quality

              The best way to separate sound quality is by oscillation energy. The three clearest types of sound come from genes with strong 48, 24 and 12 hr components. A gene with a strong 24hr component will sound an octave higher than a gene with a strong 48hr component. A gene with a strong 12hr component will sound another octave higher still. A gene with a strong 16hr component should sound a perfect fifth away from a gene with a strong 24hr component, but unfortunately there are no genes with a really strong 16hr component so the difference sounds really subtle.

              Circadian vs All Genes Sounds

              The synth sound from Circadian Mode is a lot smoother than "All Genes Mode", and that is exclusively due to the modeling method. After all, the underlying data is still the same. So instead, focus on how the sound "wobbles" over time as you hold down a note. That represents how uncertain we are of the true shape of the waveform.

              Methods

              Processing Raw Reads

              The Diurnal Dataset from Yuchen Yang and colleagues was downloaded from GSE137732. This is a bulk RNA-seq dataset from Arabidopsis grown under long-day conditions (16hr light / 8hr dark).

              The paired-end reads were processed with fastp with "--detect_adapter_for_pe". Results before and after trimming were checked using MultiQC. Fragments were mapped to the TAIR10 transcriptome and transcripts were counted using salmon, with the genome as decoy.

              DESeq2: adjusting for differences in sequencing depth and RNA composition

              DESeq2 was used to normalize the samples after using tximport to summarize transcript-level data. Normalized counts are used for sound wave generation: FFT analysis and as the input waveform for the synth. Genes with fewer than 5 average DESeq2 normalized counts across all samples were dropped.

              TPM: adjusting for differences in gene length

              Log10 of mean TPM on DESeq2 normalized counts was kept for downstream between-gene comparisons.

              VST: adjusting for heteroskedasticity

              VST was performed and the output was used for analysis of the biological signal: UMAP, Lomb-Scargle and Gaussian Process modeling.

              UMAPs

              Counts

              VST data for each gene is z-score normalized across the 16 timepoints before running UMAP with n_neighbors = 10, min_dist = 0.25, and metric = "cosine". Distance between points is (1 - Pearson correlation).

              FFT

              Each gene's normalized counts were interpolated using Catmull-Rom, mean centered, and max-absolute scaled to between -1 and 1. FFT is performed on each waveform and the resulting magnitude of each detected frequency within a gene is converted to dB relative to the strongest frequency and floored at -40dB by setting any frequency 100x weaker than the strongest frequency to 0. All dB values were shifted up by 40dB so the data ranges from 0~40dB. The resulting array is used to generate the UMAP with n_neighbors = 10, min_dist = 0.25, and metric = "euclidean".

              Limitations

              • Expression strength is currently not mapped to anything sonically, and is only used as a filter.
              • 16 points is limiting in terms of possible sounds and harmonics.
              • The same smoothing function (Catmull-Rom) is used on every waveform, so they all sound like the same family of instruments.
